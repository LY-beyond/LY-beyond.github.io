1. The three-step decision tree

The most common mistake in chart selection is “pick something pretty first, then squeeze the data into it”.

1 Why it cannot be reversed

What really decides “what a chart lets people see” is not how pretty it is, but which visual channel it uses; and whether a channel is usable is decided by the data's scale of measurement (see Chapter 1, 1.3 Data attributes).

Channel readability ranking (precision of human judgment)

Length / position > angle > area > color lightness > volume. The eye reads length most accurately and volume least accurately — so “comparing sizes with two circles” is almost guaranteed to mislead, while “comparing the lengths of two bars” almost never fails.

  • ✅ Ordinal / nominal data → length, position, color (hue only separates, never orders).
  • ⚠️ Quantitative data → position and length are the safest; area and volume only convey “order of magnitude”, never precise comparison.
  • ❌ Forcing quantitative data into angles (pie charts) → only for “≤ 5 slices, major vs minor only”.
2 The three steps

Fold the rules above into one walkable path: task → data → chart. The first two steps are constraints; the third is the choice made inside them.

3 Hands-on: walk the decision tree

Answer 2–3 questions and the wizard recommends a chart, its main visual channel, the scale it fits and its common misuses. “Back” steps back at any point, and “Start over” restarts anytime.

Section summary

  • The first step of chart selection is not “which one looks nice” but “what data do I have and what do I want to express”.
  • Channel readability: length / position > angle > area > color lightness > volume; the data's scale decides which channels are usable.
  • A chart is a solution under the “task × data” constraints, with final trade-offs by scale (category count, sample size, time span).

3. Right vs wrong charts

Same data: drawn right, the conclusion shows; drawn wrong, “the chart's lie” shows.

1 Four pairs
✗ Misuse

Seven slices crammed into one donut. Adjacent slices differ by less than 10° — the eye cannot read it; which of two similar slices is bigger is pure guesswork.

✓ Correct

Swap to a value-sorted horizontal bar chart. Length is the most precise channel — ranking and gaps read at a glance, and no number of categories feels “cramped”.

✗ Misuse

The value axis starts at 3.4, not 0. 3.4 vs 4.6 is a 35% gap, yet it looks three times bigger — once a bar chart loses its zero, its lengths stop being trustworthy.

✓ Correct

Start the bars at 0. The differences “look smaller”, but they are the true differences. To emphasize relative change, use a line chart or label percentages directly — never quietly truncate the axis.

✗ Misuse

One-dimensional values as circle areas. When the value quadruples the radius should only double, yet a reader's first instinct is “4× the radius” — visually inflated roughly 16×.

✓ Correct

Switch to length. Length maps one-to-one to value — 4× is 4×. If a “sense of size” is truly needed, the values must be labeled and the mapping rule stated.

✗ Misuse

The rainbow (jet) scale. Hue shifts while lightness jumps up and down, so readers take “the brightest yellow” as the maximum; the blue and red ends read as two extremes, inventing a midpoint that does not exist.

✓ Correct

A sequential single-hue scale (light → dark). Lightness moves with the value, so order stays readable; use a diverging scale when “deviation from a midpoint” matters (see 3.2 Choosing among three colormap families).

Section summary

  • Precise comparison by angle (pie slices), truncated value axes, and one-dimensional values encoded as area are the three stealthiest “chart lies”.
  • Bar lengths must start at 0; to emphasize relative change use a line chart or label percentages directly.
  • Color is a channel, not decoration: sequential scales for ordered data, diverging scales for deviation from a center, qualitative scales only for pure categories.

Question 1

With GDP data for 30 provincial-level regions at hand, the goal is to let readers see at a glance “who is high and who is low”, ranking them without comparing one by one. Which chart?

With 30 categories and full-length province / region names, vertical bars would crowd together. A horizontal bar chart puts the labels on the y axis with lengths starting at 0 — length, the most precise channel; sorted by value, the reader's eye only sweeps top to bottom. A pie with 30 slices is unreadable.

Question 2

A club has only 7 students and the goal is to see each student's own height. Which chart?

With only 7 samples, quartiles and extremes are unstable as statistics — a box plot would paint one student's fluke value as a group trait, and after binning a histogram holds only one or two people per bin. With tiny samples the most honest move is to draw every observation (strip plot).

Question 3

The goal is to show how the crime mix across provinces changes over time, focusing on how each crime type's share rises and falls rather than absolute counts. Which chart?

The key phrase is “share over time”. Normalizing each year to 100% leaves the stacked area's height differences showing only share changes — exactly what a 100% stacked area chart is for. Stacked bars better compare totals and composition at a few time points; a donut cannot show continuous trends.

Question 4

With 30 years of daily temperature records for one place at hand, the goal is to see “the seasonal cycle repeating”. Which chart?

30 years × 365 days ≈ ten thousand points; as a line chart they smear into a color band and the periodicity vanishes. A spiral maps the cycle onto angle (the same season always falls in the same direction) and time onto radius, so “same season, same angle, spreading outward year by year” — periodicity at a glance. A calendar heatmap fits “daily events, comparing weekday / month patterns across years”; over such a long span it gets too sparse.

Question 5

A dataset has 6 numeric variables (age, grade, height, weight, commute distance, weekly study hours) and the goal is to see their overall structure at once, not just pairwise relations. Which chart?

6 variables combine into 15 pairs — too many scatter plots to inspect one by one. A scatterplot matrix lays every pair out as a small multiple, sweeping out correlated pairs at once; parallel coordinates draw each observation as a polyline, good for overall cluster structure. Box plots treat variables one at a time and cannot show between-variable relations.

Question 6

The task is to present “each student's time at school from enrollment to graduation” — the data is an interval with start and end dates, not an instant. Which chart?

The data is an interval (start → end), not an instant. A Gantt / storyline expresses “start and end” with one segment, matching the data's nature one-to-one; a line chart crushes intervals into points and loses the span. A calendar heatmap could color the interval too, but each student occupies a whole block of cells, hiding “who stayed longer”.

After answering: take each miss back to the Section 2 quick-reference matrix — the matrix's “use with care” column is basically where these questions come from.
